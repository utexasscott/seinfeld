# Episode 3 — The Cake

**DRAW:** a piece of cake from a royal wedding / a bakery with a number system and a broken machine / regifting, and being caught by the original gifter / who pays, and what the split implies / Newman + Kramer, joint venture already in progress

**THEME:** Who pays, and what the split implies.

**A-PLOT (Jerry):** A lucite-boxed slice of cake from the 1981 royal wedding — Newman's gift, which Jerry regifted. Also: he wants out of a three-week relationship for a reason that has nothing to do with her.
**B-PLOT (Elaine):** Warren Zisk's group dinner is tonight. She is expected to bring a marble rye, her dress came back from the cleaner two sizes smaller, and there will be a bill.
**C-PLOT (George):** A soup with a strict ordering procedure, carried to a doorman building he intends to enter without calling first.
**D-PLOT (Kramer):** A six-foot cigar-store Indian arriving as freight at that same doorman building, in joint venture with Newman, on terms neither of them wrote down.

**COLLISION:** Every object on this list has to go up the same elevator, at 314 West 81st Street, past the same doorman, who will not lift anything until somebody pays. Nobody will say how to split it. Jerry cannot be on 81st Street.

## TEASER

INT. COMEDY CLUB — NIGHT

JERRY
The check arrives at the table, and suddenly everybody's an accountant. Nobody looked at a number all night — now there's forensics.

There's always the guy who throws in a twenty and says "that covers me." It does not cover him. It has never covered him.

And the one who says "let's just split it" — that's the man who ordered the lobster. That's not generosity, that's a heist with napkins.

The check isn't a bill. It's a personality test, and everybody fails it in a different direction.

INT. SCHNITZER'S BAKERY — 81ST STREET — 1:00

A six-foot cigar-store Indian stands upright on the sidewalk, wrapped in moving blankets and twine, leaning against a parking meter. NEWMAN beside it. ANIBAL behind the glass, arms folded. GEORGE arrives at a jog, holding the soup bag out in front of him like a relay baton.

NEWMAN

(LAUGHTER)

GEORGE

Newman's face changes. Very slowly, it becomes pleasant.

NEWMAN
Oh, I can put you upstairs. I go up that elevator every day at eleven and nobody asks me a thing.

The price isn't money, George. Jerry has a slice of cake in a block of lucite. I want to know where it is *right now.* Not where he says it is.

You bring me that, and Anibal never sees you. You lie to me, and I mention the soup.

GEORGE
    (without a flicker)
The cake. Sure. Everybody knows about the cake — I've *seen* the cake, Newman, I know exactly where that thing sits.

But I'm not just handing it over on a sidewalk. You walk me up first, then we discuss the cake. That's the sequence.

(BIG LAUGH)

KRAMER arrives at speed from the west and stops dead ten feet off.

KRAMER
Oh. Oh, look at this. The two of you. Shoulder to shoulder.

I send a man to *watch* my merchandise and he's standing on the same side of it as Newman.

That's not a lookout. That's a second partner. Back up, George. Back up off the blankets.

(LAUGHTER)

And now, from the east, at speed, ELAINE — who has followed a paper sleeve a block and a half.

ELAINE
George. GEORGE. You had one job, it was twenty dollars, and instead you're out here with a *tarp*.

That man just went into 314 with my rye. You're going in and you're getting it.

I don't want to hear the word "doorman." Newman — you deliver here. You have keys. Don't look at the blanket, look at me.

(BIG LAUGH)

NEWMAN
Elaine. Elaine, Elaine, Elaine. You've come to the window and you haven't brought anything to the window.

I don't do favors. I do *transactions*. Everybody on this sidewalk wants a key today and the line forms behind the soup.

Bring me something I can use. And I'll tell you right now — I already know what Jerry did with the cake. I'm simply waiting for one of you to be brave enough to say it out loud.

Elaine's face does something complicated and profitable.

(LAUGHTER)

GEORGE
The deal was *reasonable line*. You heard me say it, Kramer heard me say it — that line was around the corner. Unreasonable. The deal is void, the twenty stays with me as a kill fee.

And what you're describing now is not buying a rye. That's *recovering* a rye from a stranger's apartment. Different job, Elaine. Different price.

(BIG LAUGH)

KRAMER has stopped listening. He is already under the blankets with his arms around the thing.

KRAMER
HEY. Nobody's touched the oak. Six feet on a public sidewalk, that's a summons in ten minutes.

Soup later. George — the base. Newman, the *doors*.

Lift on three or we're all standing here explaining a chief to a patrolman. Three!

He lifts. Nobody else does. The Indian goes up about four inches and stays there, at an angle, held by one man.

ELAINE
Kill fee. You know what, George? Yada yada yada, you're dead to me.

Newman. The cake's not on a shelf anymore. He gave it away. To a *woman.* And he's dumping her tonight, which means it's in her apartment permanently.

Now go up there and get me that rye.

Newman closes his eyes for a second, the way a man does when a horse comes in.

(BIG LAUGH)

Jerry's face does nothing at all, which takes work.

(BIG LAUGH)

Kramer steps in very close to Jerry. Quiet, so only he hears it.

KRAMER
Five.

Buddy. I know what's on five. I know it better than the man who *sweeps* five.

So you go ahead and keep saying "the man."

    (turning, to the whole room)

Nothing! Lift!

(BIG LAUGH)

ELAINE
Collateral. You want my rye as *collateral.* Newman, you eat out of a mail truck.

You will pry this out of my hands and I will bite you. I have already sold out my oldest friend today for nothing — do you understand what that makes me capable of at two in the afternoon?

She shoulders past him, rye under her arm. Nobody stops her.

(BIG LAUGH)

GEORGE
Nothing in writing? Then I'm off the load. I don't work on a handshake with a man who keeps *records* — that's a one-way street, Newman.

You people fight about the bread. I have actual business in this building.

He walks out the front door, toward the fire hydrant, and the soup.

EXT. FRONT STEPS OF 314 — 1:56

The paper bag is still on the hydrant. It is empty. Folded flat.

Through the lobby glass, ANIBAL is on his stool with a soup container in one hand and a plastic spoon in the other. He is looking directly at George while he eats it. He does not stop.

GEORGE
That's mine. That's — you know that's mine, you're *looking* at me.

Nine dollars. I stood in a line where they don't let you *speak*, and you're eating it on a stool.

Enjoy it. Enjoy it! That's the last thing I ever bring into this building!

He bangs the glass once. Anibal takes another spoonful.

(BIG LAUGH)

INT. LOBBY — 1:57

Three men and an Indian on the floor.

JERRY
You know five. Fine. Then ask your partner why he knows it better.

He's got the service elevator, the mail room, two through nine, and a very specific opinion about what's empty up there. You signed with him this morning, Kramer. Sixty-forty.

I'd start with 5F. He couldn't get the words out fast enough this morning, and then he couldn't get them out at all.

Kramer goes completely still for a full second.

KRAMER
    (then, much too loud)
5F is not — that's not — that apartment is *spoken for*, alright?! It's — the man's traveling!

    (wheeling on Newman)

TWO FINGERS. That's what I want to talk about. You raised it to two and you kept the receipt and you walked off my load —

Don't you look at the ceiling. Look at ME.

(BIG LAUGH)

NEWMAN
Oh, that's *lovely.* Thirty years of this. Cornered, so he points at a stranger's door and yells "look over there."

Kramer. Kramer, look at me. He is doing it to you right now, and you're falling for it, and later you'll wonder why the cable was never hooked up.

Five F is a mailbox. The cake is a debt. Back to the debt, Jerry.

(BIG LAUGH)

Jerry looks at the two of them. He does the arithmetic, and it comes out the way it always comes out.

JERRY
Take the space. A year. It's yours.

Not the Thursdays. The Thursdays are theater, Newman, and I don't do matinees. Space for cake, and we're square — and square means done. You never bring up the cake, the hallway, the building, or anything you thought you heard in a stairwell.

Say "done" and I'll get you the paper tonight.

A pause. Newman's whole face opens like a window.

NEWMAN
I'll take the space. I'll waive the Thursdays. Look how reasonable I've become.

But you threw in the stairwell, Jerry. Nobody asked about the stairwell. I had a *coat* and a grudge, and you just told me where the money is.

The cake is settled. The window is a separate account.

He walks out the front door and turns east on 81st, the way Elaine went.

(BIG LAUGH)

KRAMER
    (pointing at the floor)
He's *lying down*. My chief is lying down on the marble and you two are trading a parking space over the body.

I brought the connection. I carried it up the steps. And I'm the only one in this lobby who doesn't know what the deal is.

That's not a partnership. That's a jury.

(BIG LAUGH)

INT. NINTH FLOOR — 9B — 2:06

GEORGE at a door, empty-handed. He knocks. Nothing. He knocks again. Somewhere inside, a television gets turned down.

GEORGE
    (to the door)
Mr. Trotta? Art Vandelay — Parks liaison, Fort Greene bandshell. I know, I know, unannounced, but this couldn't go through the office.

I brought you a soup. It was taken from me downstairs. By your own doorman. That's the *kind* of building situation I'm here about.

Two minutes. I don't even need to come in — I'll do it from the hallway.

(LAUGHTER)

The door opens.

It is the MAN IN THE GOOD COAT. From the bakery. He has a highball in his hand. He looks at George. He looks at George's empty hands.

GEORGE (CONT'D)
...Mr. Trotta. Great. Vandelay.

Now — before anything — I want to say I have no connection whatsoever to the people downstairs. The bread, the woman, the wooden Indian. Never met them.

Bandshell. Fort Greene. Can I get my name on a piece of paper here or what?

(BIG LAUGH)

INT. PARAMOUNT CLEANERS — 4:20

The dress is back on the rail. One seam let out. A visible line down the hip where the old seam was. ELAINE has the rye under one arm and has not put it down since one o'clock.

ELAINE
There's a *line*. You shrunk it, you unshrunk half of it, and now there's a line down my hip like a road.

And you're charging me. For the alteration. Of the damage. That you did.

I'm wearing it at eight o'clock in front of eight people and every one of them is going to see the line. Say "we shrunk it." One time. Then I'll pay.

He does not say it. She pays.

(BIG LAUGH)

EXT. CHIANG'S — SECOND AVENUE — 7:52 PM

ELAINE, in the dress, the seam line visible down one hip. Eight people are due at eight and Warren Zisk did not make a reservation. She sets the rye down for four seconds to fix her shoe.

She stands up. NEWMAN is holding it.

ELAINE
You followed me. You followed me *across town* for a bread.

Four seconds. My shoe came off. Four seconds and there you are, like weather.

Newman. It's seven fifty-two. Give it to me and I will never say another word about the cake as long as I live.

NEWMAN
Four seconds, Elaine. You put a marble rye on a sidewalk in New York City and you looked away for four seconds.

Here's the price. In nine minutes a truck arrives with a six-foot chief, and you are going to walk into Chiang's and tell that man his entrance has never looked better, and you're going to keep saying it until he writes a check.

Sell my Indian, and you get your bread. Otherwise I eat it right here in front of you, and it's still warm.

(BIG LAUGH)

END OF ACT TWO

## ACT THREE

INT. CHIANG'S — VESTIBULE — 8:04 PM

A crowded entryway. A podium. A wall of coats. Warren Zisk's party of eight is somewhere in the crush and there is no table for them, because there is no reservation. Through the front window: a rented van at the curb, doors open, and KRAMER backing out of it with the head end of a wrapped Indian. GEORGE has the base and will not let go of it.

Thirty people waiting for tables press back against the wall to let six feet of oak come through the door.

KRAMER
    (walking it upright into the corner, arms wide)
THERE. Right there by the podium. Now you got an *entrance*.

Look at him. Six feet of solid oak greeting every party of four that walks through that door. That's not decor, people, that's a landmark.

You'll be "the place with the Indian" by Thursday. Giddyup!

(LAUGHTER)

GEORGE
    (still holding the base)
Say the number, Kramer. Out loud. Right here, in front of all these people — what's my end?

*Ten percent.* Everybody hear that? Ten percent to the man on the base! You're all witnesses, you people in the vestibule, you heard it!

I'm not letting go of this thing till somebody hands me money.

(BIG LAUGH)

NEWMAN moves Elaine forward by the elbow. The rye is under his arm. Somewhere behind them, eight people from her office are watching her do this.

ELAINE
Mr. Chiang. Hi. Hello. This —

    (gesturing at the Indian)

— is an authentic nineteenth-century American piece and it would look tremendous right there by your fish tank.

It's a conversation starter. People wait forty minutes for a table here. This gives them something to *do*.

Nine hundred. I'm not the owner. I'm — I'm a friend of the piece.

(BIG LAUGH)

The front door opens. JERRY, with an envelope, taking in the entire vestibule at once.

JERRY
Newman. Envelope. Space is yours, we're square.

You people got a rye, an Indian, and a dress. That's not a dinner, that's a pawn shop.

I'm not sitting down.

(LAUGHTER)

Newman takes the envelope. Checks it. Pockets it. Then, kindly:

NEWMAN
Signed. Dated. That's a pleasure to receive from you.

Don't go, Jerry. Sit down, have a spring roll. Something came out of that building today in my bag, and I think you're going to want to be in the room when I open it.

He pats the bag. Jerry does not leave.

(BIG LAUGH)

JERRY (CONT'D)
We're square, Newman. Square means I go home.

    (not going home)

...I'll stand. I'm standing. I'm not sitting, I'm just — near the bag.

(LAUGHTER)

A heavyset man from the crowd, name tag, two coats over his arm, asks George who he is and how he's involved with the piece.

GEORGE
Vandelay. Art Vandelay. I handle the *placement* end — architecture and public installation, mostly Parks work, the Fort Greene bandshell is mine.

This piece? Twenty-two at Sotheby's in June. I moved it personally today.

You want a card, I'm out. Gave my last one to a client uptown.

(LAUGHTER)

The name goes down the line of coats. It comes back wrong.

Three people from Elaine's office turn and look at George with enormous warmth. WARREN ZISK comes out of the crush with his hand already extended, saying the name he has been waiting all night to say.

He is saying *Vanoy.*

ELAINE
    (one beat; the whole decision happens in it)
...Warren. Warren, you found him.

He doesn't shake hands. It's a whole — it's a discipline thing, you don't ask, don't ask him about it.

And no photographs. Warren. Warren, put the phone down. PUT IT DOWN.

(BIG LAUGH)

George does not ask a single question.

GEORGE
No, no — she's right. I don't shake. Nothing personal, it's a *discipline* thing. I've kept it eleven years.

And no photographs. Not of me, not of the piece. That's how these things walk away.

    (to a woman from Accounts)

You. You've got a nice face. You may nod at me.

(BIG LAUGH)

At the podium: MR. CHIANG looks at the Indian. He looks at the ceiling height. He writes a number on the back of a takeout menu and slides it across.

KRAMER
    (covering the menu with his whole hand, low)
One-*forty*. Elaine. The doorman took two hundred. We are underwater on a doorman.

    (bright, over his shoulder)

George! Nothing! Beautiful number, don't come over here!

    (back down, quiet)

Ten percent of this is fourteen dollars and he thinks it's a car.

(BIG LAUGH)

And then Newman opens the bag.

He lifts out a block of lucite with a piece of cake in it and holds it up at shoulder height, in a crowded vestibule, four feet from Jerry.

NEWMAN
Charles and Diana. Nineteen eighty-one.

You gave it away, you begged a door for it, you bought me off with a parking space — and it came back down that elevator on its own and got handed to me with the outgoing mail. For *free*, Jerry. Postage due: nothing.

That's what I am. Everything in that building eventually passes through my hands.

(BIG LAUGH)

George's hands are still on the base of the Indian. His face changes.

GEORGE
The *cake*? It was the cake the whole time?! I've been carrying a tree around Manhattan since one o'clock!

I found that cake! I had it on the sidewalk — I priced it, I *brokered* it, and she gave it away for nothing!

That's my ten percent, Newman! Ten percent of the cake!

He lets go.

Six feet of solid oak goes over sideways into a room full of people waiting for tables. It catches Newman's raised arm on the way down. The lucite block comes out of his hand, hits the tile, and splits.

The Indian lands across the coat rack. The headdress snaps off at the neck.

Silence in the vestibule. Thirty strangers, a broken chief, and four pieces of royal wedding cake on the floor of a Chinese restaurant.

ELAINE
Twenty-two —

    (she has to put a hand on the podium)

Twenty-two thousand dollars.

I gave that away on a sidewalk. For free. To *you*. And I still don't have the bread. I STILL DON'T HAVE THE BREAD.

Ugghhh. And a hundred and forty for the Indian. I'm down four hundred dollars, I'm wearing a line, and there's a priest.

(BIG LAUGH)

KRAMER
    (on his knees in the coats, holding the headdress in two hands)
Ohhhh. Oh, no. No, no, no — his *head*, George. His head came off in a coat rack.

Solid oak. Do you know what it takes to snap solid oak? It takes a Costanza.

    (to Mr. Chiang, holding it up, hopeful)

...You could hang this part. Separate. Two pieces, same money.

(BIG LAUGH)

JERRY
Twenty-two thousand dollars. On the tile. By the umbrella stand.

You should've left it on my shelf, Newman. Nine years, nothing ever happened to it.

A waiter arrives with a broom.

NEWMAN
    (quiet, looking down at it)
Don't sweep. Put the broom down and back away from it.

Nine years it was sealed. Nine years, and it survives a wedding, a shelf, a regift, a stranger's apartment... and it dies on tile, in a vestibule, because George Costanza got complimented.

He kneels. He picks up the largest piece. He eats it.

NEWMAN (CONT'D)
...It's dry. Nine years and it's *dry.*

(BIG LAUGH)

MR. CHIANG has not said a word this entire time. He takes the takeout menu with 140 written on it, turns it over, writes a new number, and turns it around so everyone can see.

It says **310.**

Coat rack. Tile. The pump on the fish tank.

KRAMER
    (standing, brushing coats off himself, businesslike)
Alright. Alright, this is clean. Damages follow the hands. The hands were George's.

Ten percent, buddy. You wanted ten percent of the venture — this *is* the venture. Ten percent of nothing, a hundred percent of the fish tank.

Newman, get the head. We're leaving.

He goes out the front door with the body of the Indian.

(BIG LAUGH)

GEORGE
I'm the *base* man. I was never the head man. Whoever's on the head is responsible for the head — that's how a two-man carry works, ask anybody in this room.

And I was misrepresented on the cargo. I was told twenty-two thousand. You misstate the value, the whole arrangement is void.

Three-ten goes to the owner. I don't own a splinter of that thing.

(LAUGHTER)

ELAINE
No no no. There's no split. The three-ten is MINE. Every dollar of it, right off the top, against the two-seventy I already put in.

You want the forty left over? Take it, fight over it, choke on it.

And the rye comes to me *now*, Newman. Before that menu changes hands.

(BIG LAUGH)

NEWMAN
Mr. Chiang. The chief's body left the building, so we're renegotiating. One head, solid oak, historic — against your three hundred and ten in damages. We're square, and I'll throw in a genuine two-hundred-and-seventy-dollar marble rye for the front table.

Elaine, take it up with the restaurant. I've settled my account.

He sets the head on the podium. He lays the rye beside it. He walks out.

Mr. Chiang looks at the head. Looks at the rye. Nods once.

(BIG LAUGH)

JERRY
Well. Goodnight, everybody. Split it however you want — you're all so good with a check.

I'd offer you a ride, but I don't have anywhere to put the car.

He exits.

(BIG LAUGH)

A busboy carries the rye through the kitchen doors on a tray.

ELAINE
That's my rye. That is my *rye*, it just went through swinging doors on a tray.

Two hundred and seventy dollars, a headdress, and a wooden man, and I'm walking into that dining room empty-handed with a line down my hip.

    (to George, low, through her teeth)

You sit at the head of that table and you do not speak. You bless the food and that is *it*.

(BIG LAUGH)

INT. CHIANG'S — DINING ROOM — 8:30 PM

A table for eight, finally. The heavyset man with the name tag pulls out the chair at the head and gestures George into it.

GEORGE
    (sitting)
...Head of the table. Sure. If that's where you people need me, that's where I'll sit.

I've been up since six and I haven't had a thing all day. Somebody lost my soup.

Order whatever you want. Get the shrimp. Get *two* shrimp. And the check comes to me — and then I hand it directly to a gentleman named Kramer.

(BIG LAUGH)

Warren Zisk stands, glass in hand, and announces warmly that to keep it simple everybody should just split the check evenly — and that of course Vanoy doesn't pay.

ELAINE
Even. Sure. Except I had soup and Warren had the *duck*, and now I'm buying a third of a duck I didn't eat.

And he doesn't pay? He's the only one at this table who ordered two entrees.

I brought the bread, Warren. I brought a two-hundred-and-seventy-dollar bread. Where do you think it went?

(BIG LAUGH)

Warren asks the table to bow their heads. Eight heads go down. Everyone waits.

Elaine's eyes close, and not in prayer.

GEORGE
Bless this food. Bless this table. Bless everyone at it who has never once asked a man to carry something.

And Lord — watch over the ones who take what isn't theirs. The doormen. You know who you are.

Amen. Let's eat.

(BIG LAUGH)

EXT. SECOND AVENUE — 8:50 PM

A headless six-foot cigar-store Indian propped against a mailbox. KRAMER beside it with his hand flat on its shoulder, looking up the avenue. NEWMAN comes out of the restaurant with nothing in his hands.

KRAMER
Van's gone. Van went at eight.

So that's a headless Indian, a mailbox, and the two of us. On Second Avenue. In August.

    (brightening)

He can stay here overnight. Nobody steals a torso, Newman. There's no market.

(LAUGHTER)

NEWMAN
Look at us, Kramer. Out every dollar, two hundred to a man who won't speak, and our chief is headless against a mailbox.

He pats his coat pocket.

NEWMAN (CONT'D)
But I've got Jerry's parking space in writing for one year. Every single day he circles this block, and I don't.

And there's still a window on 81st Street nobody's paid for. Good night, Kramer.

He walks east, laughing. Kramer stands alone on Second Avenue with a headless man.

(BIG LAUGH)

END OF ACT THREE

## TAG

INT. COMEDY CLUB — NIGHT

JERRY
Here's what nobody tells you about the check. Sometimes you pay it and you don't even eat.

You hand over the money to make a thing go away, and the thing goes away — and then you're standing there with no thing and no money.

The worst part is you can't complain, because you agreed. That's what a check is. It's a receipt for a decision you already regret.

Everybody at that table is going home even. Except the guy who paid for the quiet. He's the only one who bought something he can't show you.

(BIG LAUGH)

**END OF EPISODE**

## Note

"Six weeks ago, on this street? I saw you. I saw you and I looked in a window." So Jerry, alone in a hallway, confesses — sincerely, unprompted, to a closed door with nobody around to force it. You've now spent three straight episodes quietly making the man honest, and this one also solves the hello runner for the audience so nobody has to hold two ideas at once. And that's the third tag in a row where he stands at a brick wall and tells the crowd what the episode was about; if the story worked you don't need the summary, and if it didn't the summary won't save it.
